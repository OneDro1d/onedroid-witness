Business models in the agentic age: a brief (part 1 of 2)
Business models in the agentic age (part 1 of 2)
In 2 parts, each with its own claims and sources at the bottom: part 1 · part 2.
A brief for founders, operators and advisers of small technical teams. It argues that producing software has become cheap while judgement has not, that the margin now sits with whoever makes agents work inside a specific business and carries the risk, and that a small independent team can earn that margin by building a process once and operating it under a named human, at a flat fee, with every action on the record. Figures carry a source number; the brief's own arithmetic is marked as such. Sources are at the end.
1. What changed
Producing software got cheaper. Judgement did not. That is this brief's argument: language models made the first working version of most business software cheap, while deciding what to build, wiring it into a customer's systems and data, and carrying the blame when it fails did not get cheaper. The evidence that fits it: a16z's "The Palantirization of everything" (January 2026) reports that enterprise implementation "often requires lots of handholding" and that postings for forward-deployed engineers rose 800 to 1,000% [1]. On this reading, value moved to the person who makes the software work inside one business, and to the party who carries the risk.
The margin gap. A services firm keeps about a third of revenue after direct costs [2]. Classic software keeps three quarters or more [4][5][6]. AI software sits in between [3], and is projected to rise [7].

Accenture already sells half of its revenue as managed services: $34.57 billion of $69.67 billion in fiscal 2025, at the company-wide 31.9% margin [2]. The question for any services firm is whether agents doing the work would move that number.

Business models in the agentic age: a brief (part 2 of 2)
Business models in the agentic age (part 2 of 2)
In 2 parts, each with its own claims and sources at the bottom: part 1 · part 2.
4. The recommendation
Start with (b): build then operate. A fixed fee for a first working version in about two weeks, credited if the build goes ahead. Then a monthly build fee with a minimum term, and a flat operations fee once the process runs. Use every engagement to build one reusable operating layer the team owns: the runner, the monitoring and the record. Keep (h) as the second step: if the record is something a bank, insurer or auditor will rely on, sell the record, not the hours. Where the reusable layer becomes a product others install, price it as (d), with seats for the humans who judge and endpoints for the agents.
The unit is a supervised process. The operations fee is priced per process and per environment, between the software price and the human price. The supervision ratio is the margin lever. Take a supervisor who does the work of five and charges what three would cost. Labour is 1 against a price of 3, so 67% before inference and tooling. At the 23% inference share that ICONIQ reports [3], the margin falls to about 44%. Getting back above 50% needs inference under about 15% of the price, or a price nearer four; 60% at a price of 3 would need inference near 7%.

The supervision arithmetic. This is the brief's own calculation, not a measurement. Only the inference share is sourced [3]. We found no audited margin for a supervised-agent service; the figures that exist are self-reported.
Guardrails. A usage band in every flat fee, with a pass-through above it. A written list of what "operate" excludes: new features are a new build fee. Capped liability. A record of every agent action, which also feeds (h). Add, never replace: the automation sits next to the client's systems and never replaces what cannot be reversed, so the client can switch it off and go back to the old way. The switch is a pause that a named person or a rule can pull, and a human restarts. The judgement stays with a named human. Nothing in the offer replaces it.
When the recommendation flips. A capital partner buys a services firm in your domain and keeps control: (f), with a minority stake as upside. Several clients want the same agent: (d) or (c). A clean, countable result at volume: (c). Outside users adopt your runtime unprompted: (e). A risk-bearer asks for your records: (h) becomes primary. Cash runs short before operations fees start: (a), which pays fastest.
5. Why a client needs an independent provider
Three reasons, and each is about judgement, not technology.
Technology bias. Advice on agents often comes from someone who sells the model, the platform or the agents under them, and two of the largest consultancies have tied themselves to vendors. Accenture made OpenAI one of its primary AI partners for its next generation of services in December 2025 [15]. EY and Microsoft put more than a billion US dollars over five years into a joint initiative in May 2026 [16]. A firm with that commitment has answered "which model, which platform" before it has heard the client's process. An independent provider sells none of these. It picks the model, the platform or no agent at all, process by process, and changes its mind when the field moves.
Asking the right questions. The expensive mistake is not a bad model. It is automating a process nobody should have kept, or automating the judgement instead of the busywork. The first weeks go on the questions a vendor skips: which steps are repetition and which are decisions; who answers when the agent is wrong; what the auditor, the bank or the customer will need to see afterwards; what the business does while the agent is paused. A team with no product to place can ask these without a preferred answer.
People in the driving seat. Agents are good at the work everyone hates and poor at judging. That is the provider's position, and the design follows from it: a named person sits in the driving seat for every process, the agents do the cases, the person decides the exceptions, and the record shows who decided what. The provider never offers to replace human judgement.
A small, fast-moving team of professionals with agents has no quota to fill for a vendor and no hidden agenda. It earns its fee when the process runs and the client can prove it did. That is what lets it represent the client's interest against the vendor, including recommending less automation than the vendor would like.
6. The offer on one page
What the provider sells. Auditable automation: agents do a company's repetitive business process, a named person judges the results, and every action is recorded so someone who was not there can replay it. A plane fitted with a black box is the picture. The aim is to remove the work everyone hates, not the people. The deliverable is a running system and its record, never a discovery report.
Who the client is. A company without a dedicated internal software product team, whose next financing, contract, audit or customer depends on software it cannot build or prove itself, with a date attached. The trigger sells, not the sector. Warm introductions come first, in any industry. Companies whose counterparty is a bank, insurer or auditor become the focus once there is a track record.
The deal shape. Services only. A fixed-fee first version in about two weeks, credited if the build goes ahead. A monthly build fee with a minimum term, planned in months. Then the operations fee, per process and per environment, with the record as a separate line per environment. A provider quotes each engagement on its own. No equity in clients, unless a capital partner the provider knows holds control and the stake is upside on top of fees. The model assumes a provider bootstrapped from fees, with no raise.
Two contract guardrails. The automation is added next to the client's systems and never replaces what cannot be reversed. The switch is a pause that a named person or a rule can pull, and a human restarts.
People. People are brought on when a revenue stream covers them. An engagement can carry a lead time so the provider can bring people onboard before delivery starts. This brief's judgement: two people plus agents run a handful of engagements, not thirty.
What breaks it. 1. The flat fee hides cost growth as volume and inference rise. 2. "Operate" turns into "keep building"; a written list of what operate excludes prevents it. 3. The provider carries liability for a process it does not control. 4. Revenue grows only with the client count. 5. The one-to-five ratio is an assumption about how much one person can verify in an hour, unmeasured until the first clients. 6. A record issued by the team that built the product is worth nothing: the sealed test scenarios must come from the counterparty or from a separate custodian.
Two early measures. Gross margin per engagement from day one, against the arithmetic in section 4. And verification minutes per agent-hour on the first two clients, which decides whether the one-to-five ratio is real.
7. The equity question
Be a supplier or take control. Take a minority stake only when a known capital partner holds control and the stake is upside on top of cash fees. A funded investor with a portfolio can hold stakes at scale: Y Combinator's standard deal is $125,000 for 7% plus $375,000 on an uncapped SAFE with most-favoured-nation terms [17]. A supplier whose money and contracts are tied together cannot: Palantir agreed to put $405.5 million into nearly two dozen SPAC targets in 2021, companies that then bought Palantir software, and they produced 2% of its revenue in the first three quarters [13]. A minority stake without control is exposure without a lever, and equity bends advice. A small warrant on top of full cash fees, with no board seat, costs little. The line that matters is no equity in place of cash.
